# Value (EVE)

Economic value of equity is what the bank's future cash flows are worth today, assets less liabilities, read under each rate scenario. This page covers how Studio computes it, the discount bases, the standardized shocks, and the Quillfield figures behind Step 2 and two of the More demos.

## The idea and why a bank cares

Net interest income looks a year or two ahead. Value looks at the whole life of the book, discounted back to today, so it catches what earnings miss: a 30-year mortgage pool that will pay a below-market coupon for decades after rates rise, or a deposit franchise that will keep paying far less than the market rate. A bank that only watched earnings could be perfectly hedged for the next twelve months and still have lost a large part of its economic worth. That is why the international standard for interest rate risk in the banking book sets its outlier test on the change in economic value under standardized shocks, and why boards see both measures on the same page. The US has not adopted that standard, so on this site the six shocks are a reference, not a requirement.

## The method as Studio computes it

Each position's monthly cash flows are projected under the scenario's rate path, with the behavior that path implies: mortgages prepay faster when rates fall, deposit balances leave faster when the market rate runs ahead of the paid rate, certificates of deposit are broken early when the offered rate rises above the contract rate. Those cash flows are then discounted on the scenario's own curve. A cash flow in month $k$ (counting from one) is discounted at $t = k/12$:

$$\mathrm{PV} = \sum_{k} \mathrm{DF}\!\left(\tfrac{k}{12}\right)\,(\text{principal}_k + \text{interest}_k), \qquad \mathrm{EVE} = \mathrm{PV}_{\text{assets}} - \mathrm{PV}_{\text{liabilities}} + \mathrm{PV}_{\text{derivatives}}.$$

The swap is discounted on the risk-free curve whatever basis the rest of the book uses, and its value is added last as the derivatives line. An undrawn line has no value and sits on neither side. A balance held at book (the non-earning assets) is counted at par in every scenario. The change against base is reported in dollars and as a fraction of base value, which the screens show as a percent.

The curve is bootstrapped from the snapshot's Treasury par yields on the semi-annual convention, with zero rates continuously compounded and discount factors interpolated log-linearly between quoted nodes. A parallel shock adds its size to every zero rate. A custom shock vector is read at each monthly node's tenor, linear between the knots and flat beyond them.

## Conventions and their choices

**Discount basis.** Value is read on the risk-free curve by default, which is the Treasury curve alone. The all-in basis adds the snapshot's term liquidity premium, held static across shocks, so every cash flow is discounted at the bank's own funding curve. The spread-consistent basis goes one step further and adds each loan family's illustrative credit spread (one round value per family, stated in the bank file, not the family's pricing margin); deposits carry no spread on any basis. The readings differ in level, and they can differ in sign: at -200 bp risk-free value falls while the all-in and spread-consistent readings rise (below). I chose risk-free as the default because it is the one a learner can reproduce from a published curve, and because the funding premium and the spreads on this bank are constructed values, not market quotes.

**Run-off, not going concern.** Value is read on the existing book running down, with nothing replacing it. New volume enters value only through the separate going-concern view on the Reports step, which this guide does not quote.

**Behavior re-levels under each shock.** Cash flows are regenerated per scenario rather than shifted, so the mortgage option and the deposit option show up as changes in the cash flows themselves. This is what makes value concave in the shock: a thinly capitalized, option-heavy book can lose value on both sides of base, and on Quillfield's risk-free reading it does (below).

**The six standardized shocks.** With $S(t) = e^{-t/4}$ and $t$ the tenor in years, the parallel shocks are plus and minus 200 bp at every tenor; the short-rate shocks are plus and minus $300\,S(t)$ bp; the long-rate shock, $225\,(1 - S(t))$ bp, is used only inside the two twists; the steepener is $-0.65\,|\text{short}| + 0.9\,|\text{long}|$ and the flattener $+0.8\,|\text{short}| - 0.6\,|\text{long}|$. The sizes are the USD ones after the July 2024 recalibration, which raised the long-rate shock from 150 to 225 bp. The replica computes these vectors itself from the published formula and holds the seeded setup to them.

**The deposit convention.** The behavioral convention runs each deposit on its decay and beta model. The regulatory convention splits each deposit into a stable core, laid out as ten equal rungs with a five-year average life at today's paid rate, and a non-core slice that leaves in one month. The engine applies one core cap and one life to every deposit, where the standard sets different caps and lives by deposit class. [`deposits.md`](https://github.com/chihchen22/alm-studio/blob/main/docs/guide/deposits.md) has the detail.

A parallel rise of 200 bp takes about <!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.risk-free.S200.deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$196 million off risk-free value, <!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.risk-free.S200.deltaEvePct scale=percent dp=1 sign=abs -->14.9 percent. A parallel fall of 200 bp also costs value, about <!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.risk-free.S-200.deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$27 million (<!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.risk-free.S-200.deltaEvePct scale=percent dp=1 sign=abs -->2.0 percent): the mortgages and the agency MBS refinance into the rally, so the asset side gains less than a bullet book would, while the liability side rises in value, most of it the checking deposits, and the pay-fixed swap loses. Read against the smaller moves (down about <!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.risk-free.S100.deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$89 million at +100 bp, up about <!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.risk-free.S-100.deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$37 million at -100 bp), risk-free value is highest about 100 bp below today's rates and falls away on both sides of that point. On the all-in basis the same fall of 200 bp adds about <!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.all-in.S-200.deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$32 million, and on the spread-consistent basis about <!-- num target=demo-spread-consistent-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=S-200].deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$49 million. On those higher discount curves the mortgage book sits closer to par, so prepaying at par in the rally costs it less and the asset side gains more; the funding premium also trims the liabilities' gain, and the loan spreads act on the assets only. The down shock turns from a loss into a gain.

Under the six standardized shocks, parallel up is the worst row, the same $196 million. Short rates up costs about <!-- num target=demo-six-shocks-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=SRP-SUP].deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$111 million, the flattener about <!-- num target=demo-six-shocks-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=SRP-FLAT].deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$86 million and the steepener about <!-- num target=demo-six-shocks-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=SRP-STEEP].deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$79 million; parallel down costs the $27 million above, and short rates down is the one row that adds value, about <!-- num target=demo-six-shocks-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=SRP-SDN].deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$55 million. Note that both twists cost value, for different reasons. Under the steepener the long end rises, the mortgages and the agency MBS lose more than the checking deposits' value as liabilities falls, and the bank is worse off. Under the flattener the long end falls, so the long-lived checking deposits are worth more as liabilities, and that rise outweighs what the assets lose.

On the regulatory deposit convention base value is about <!-- num target=demo-regulatory-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=S0].eve scale=million dp=0 unit=$ -->$1,114 million, about <!-- num target=quillfield-v1 cell=eve.risk-free.S0.eve minus=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=S0].eve minus-target=demo-regulatory-v1 scale=million dp=0 unit=$ -->$205 million below the behavioral reading, and the +200 bp loss roughly halves to about <!-- num target=demo-regulatory-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=S200].deltaEveVsBase scale=million dp=0 unit=$ sign=abs -->$98 million (<!-- num target=demo-regulatory-v1 cell=eve.byScenario[scenarioId=S200].deltaEvePct scale=percent dp=1 sign=abs -->8.8 percent). The convention gives the deposits less value at base because it sends the non-core slice out in a month and lays the core out in rungs with a five-year average life, both shorter than the behavioral decay, so the deposits pay below market for less time. Under a rise the same layout works the other way: the rungs' timing and paid rate do not respond to the shock, so the deposits' value as liabilities falls by far more than it does on the behavioral convention, and that larger fall offsets more of the asset loss. Base value and protection therefore move in opposite directions. Neither reading is the true one; they are two conventions about an unobservable.
